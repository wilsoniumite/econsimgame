**Landed: the first four mechanisms of the game's rules** (each built from a probe's prototype, reviewed and attacked by a second helper, then merged by a third with a six-seed check before and after; each has a page under `docs/rules/` with its numbers, what the sweeps showed and what worries us). All are off under the port's rules, and the golden test still holds the port in place.

- *Prices* (`docs/rules/prices.md`): merchants mark a price above or below a slow anchor by the stock they hold, and stop buying into a glut. This is the cure for the 8-year swings in iron and the 3-year zigzag in farm employment, which were one oscillator in the price rule. It also closes the hole where merchants paid the gate price for everything a works made.
- *Engel* (`docs/rules/engel.md`): as a town's real income rises, households' marginal budget shares move smoothly from the content's shares toward a richer set (less food and warmth, more comfort), and the observer gains chained price and real-wage series beside the fixed 1750 basket.
- *Machines* (`docs/rules/machines.md`): farms buy machinery where it pays instead of all at once (no more machinery famine), machines keep improving with the steel made and reach the quarries and clay pits, and converters and steel rails are made to pay (a new method inherits part of its predecessor's skill; a steel rail outlasts iron ones).
- *Harvest* (`docs/rules/harvest.md`): each year's harvest is better or worse than expected, island-wide and by district, from a random stream of its own, with news of poor years, bumper years and dearths.

Together, on eight islands with six families against the port (`isostate-core sweep --seeds 1-8 --rules game --families 6`): the real wage is 1.44, 1.86 and 2.46 times its 1750 level in 1800, 1850 and 1900 (the port: 1.31, 1.35, 1.35); output per head peaks in 1891, not 1858; the share of people in towns reaches 0.43 by 1900 (0.26); converter steel is first made in 1846 on average and is 99 per cent of steel by 1880 (never); the yearly swing of the iron price falls by a third and the farm zigzag by three fifths; downturn alarms fall from 15 a run to 8. What got worse: famine notices rise from 2.5 a run to 4.2, because households on the Engel curve eat nearer the floor and harvests now vary. The merging itself found a fault no single mechanism had: a town founded late expected 1750's prices for its first year, which with cheap late-century grain sent its wage and then the island's prices running; a new town now takes its mother town's expectations. Works are still as many and as small as before (6,900 by 1900): the life cycle for works is the next to merge, and kept town ground follows it.

**Landed: town ground that is kept** (`docs/rules/towns.md`). A town keeps an ordered list of cells from its centre out; a cell's room rises with its age; when the people exceed the room the town takes its best free neighbour, so it grows in ribbons along roads and water; it gives a cell up only after a long, deep decline; works stand on town ground. On eight islands with six families the towns hold 62 cells in 1750, 97 in 1800, 177 in 1850 and 305 in 1900 (before: 62, 50, 77, 122), the churn of town ground over ten years falls from 8 per cent to under half of one, and works ground falls again to 165 cells. It costs a little: the real wage of 1900 is 2.59 times 1750's, not 2.80, and farm land is 2 per cent less. The record gains, for the viewer, the year each cell joined its town, its residents, and the share of every cell under works. The viewer does not use them yet, and it shows: the home town of 1900 is now a wide pale ground with houses strung along some streets and thinly dotted elsewhere. Drawing young outer town ground as what it is (lanes, closes and a few houses among fields) is under way now that the house kit and the far view are in.

**Landed: a life cycle for works, railways that keep growing, carting that takes labour, and people who answer to how they live** (four more mechanisms of the game's rules, each with its page: `docs/rules/works.md`, `railways.md`, `carting.md`, `people.md`).

- *Works.* A works has finished building when little of its order is left (so it may grow again the next year, not six years on); it takes land by its hands and grows in place up to its cell; entry counts the merchant's spread, so the return promised is the one a works gets; each town and trade has a year's room for new capacity, incumbents first; a growing family branch extends its works instead of founding another; and hands walk to work, so light trades stand in and by the town while furnaces stay on the coal.
- *Railways.* Lines branch from any junction to towns without track and to the districts with the largest carting bills, each priced on the carting it saves over the whole network; a railway costs several times what it did and takes two years; promoters see the order book for rails. *Carting* takes hands out of a town's labour supply, so that better roads and railways free people for other work: this is what makes transport matter to the standard of living.
- *People.* Growth answers to real income, crowding and dearth instead of sitting at its cap, and towns are founded all century where work gathers far from a market, with a catchment of a decent size.

Together with the first five, on eight islands with six families against the port: 699 works in 1900 where there were 6,973, the middling one five and a half times the efficient scale and the largest 119 times; 222 cells of works ground where there were 981; railways at 326 km in 1830, 1,308 in 1850, 2,109 in 1870 and 2,619 in 1900, still growing in 1898 (the port stops at about 1,070 in 1850); the last town founded in 1840, not 1802, though there are fewer towns early (13 in 1800 against 19); the real wage 1.61, 1.99 and 2.80 times its 1750 level in 1800, 1850 and 1900; the share of people in towns 0.41 by 1900; family news a third of what it was. A game's player ends with a few dozen works in place of several hundred. Still to settle in the calibration pass: output per head peaks in 1885 on average, downturn alarms are up a quarter on the port (18.6 a run), and the grain price swings a fifth more. On the map the home town of 1900 is now several districts of houses with the railway through them and the grey plain has shrunk to a few squares of bare works ground; the summit's grey mass of pits and quarries is the mines mechanism's to mend, and town ground that is kept is in review.

**What five probes found** (each measured on 8 to 16 seeds and prototyped a cure; the reports are in `docs/probes/`):

- *Why thousands of tiny works* (`dx-firms.md`). A works counts as "still building" until under a millionth of its order is left, which takes five to seven years, and a building works may not grow; it also stops at four times its efficient scale. So demand is met by ever more small works: 4,300 by 1900, or 6,800 with families, whose branches found a small works a year. And a works of efficient scale takes a quarter of a 2 km cell whatever its trade, which is the grey plain. A life cycle of six small rules gives about 575 works in 1900, a few large ones in each town and trade, and about 100 industry cells instead of 850 to 970.
- *Why towns do not grow on the map* (`dx-towns.md`). The share of people in towns is an accounting identity on works' labour, the count of town cells stays flat because density is made to rise as fast as the people, and the churn of town ground is arithmetic noise. A kept layer of town ground, with works taking land by their hands, grows town ground about threefold by 1900 with almost no churn.
- *Why history stops* (`dx-growth.md`). It stops about 1800, not 1850: nothing raises productivity on the land after that and three quarters of labour works the land. Farm machinery then arrives as a machinery famine (the dip of the 1870s). Converters and steel rail mills are found and never built because they can never pay, and steel has no use that is not already met. The fixed 1750 basket also understates the real wage (1.36 in 1900 where a chained index reads 1.71).
- *Where the price swings and the farm zigzag come from* (`dx-cycles.md`). One mechanism, in the price rule: the price is the running sum of past stock gaps and the stock the running sum of past surpluses, an oscillator with almost no friction. Letting merchants mark prices by the stock they hold removes the zigzag and cuts the iron swings by about 40 per cent; capacity pipelines, slower investment and the land clocks do not.
- *Why the railways stop* (`dx-rail.md`). The promoters run out of candidates, not of money: every town is joined by 1850 and the mineral-line rule only looks at cells already on the line. Unbuilt branches would return hundreds of per cent. And railways do not raise output per head at all, because carting is paid to households as income and uses nobody's time.

## 2026-11-09 (evening): families v2, the family interface, towns and a map pass

Stella's direction after the first playable game: where the simulation plays well (placing buildings), keep the player out; give the player artificial, higher-level limits instead (one trade to start, the family's people as the limit on where it can act, research later), a Crusader Kings style family with a head, and no sliders: every choice discrete, without jank. The player plays the family as a whole, the head's location gives power there, there is no information fog, and marriages and alliances are out for now. Land buying is undecided.

**Families v2 in the core** (helper, branch `families-v2`, merged; `crates/isostate-core/FAMILIES.md` has the rules and the balance tables). Members have posts: child, learning, manager of a trade, head, or none. At fourteen a child is apprenticed in a trade at a town (the family learns the trade when the apprenticeship ends) or sent to university (much abler, and educated). A family knows the trades its people have learned; it starts with one. A branch is a trade in a town with someone of the family there to run it, a manager living there or the head at the seat; it grows (a works a year, or land for estates), holds or winds down, and its works get a bonus that rises with the runner's skill. Works with no branch over them are run by a hired manager for a fifth of their profit. The seat is the only place the player builds works or buys land directly; moving it, and posting people, takes effect at the year end. Members age, have children and die; when the head dies the eldest adult heads the family and the player may choose another. Every choice that waits for the player is a decision with a default, taken at the end of the next year if unanswered. AI families play by the same rules. With `n_families = 0` the core is still byte for byte the port.

Balance after the helper's pass (scripted players over four seeds): a player who takes every default finishes 1st to 4th of six, within 10% of the best rival; a thoughtful player wins three seeds of four; a landlord who puts three quarters of its cash into land around the seat wins all four. The spending rule now draws on cash only (70% of last month's income plus 1% of cash a month), so families save about a quarter to a third of their income. Open points it found: land beats works; families own 57% of the works' capital by 1900 in seed 7; industrial rivals stay small; decisions come often; AI families never hold, wind down or move.

**The family interface.** Decisions open as cards that stop the clock, each option saying what it would mean (the trade's prospects in that town, the year an apprentice brings a new trade home); Decide later leaves them for a badge beside Next year. The Family panel has the worth and accounts, anything waiting (decisions, members with nothing to run), the family as a tree with the dead marked, and the great families, each of which opens to show its head, trades and branches. Clicking a member lists where they would count most: works or land with nobody of the family over them first, then the towns where a known trade would earn most. Holdings lists the branches with Grow, Hold and Wind down. A town's note offers to move the seat there and to post anyone free to run a trade there. Every family's seat flies its flag on the market square, and the news can be filtered to the great families.

**A map pass** (helper, branch `map-pass`, merged; the viewer README describes it in full). Relief below the scale of a cell (swells in the lowland, spurs and V-shaped valleys in the hills, crests in the mountains); rivers that meander as sine-generated curves in valleys cut to a falling water level, with floodplains, estuaries, stone bridges and causeways; ragged coasts with beaches and cliffs, and a sea with its own shader reading the depth; fields cut from a warped grid into long hedge lines and near-parallel blocks, small round the towns and large on the hills, with stone walls on high ground; open strip fields round each town until its parish is enclosed in a year of its own between 1755 and 1835, when hedges, hedgerow trees and farmsteads appear; and railways graded to a ruling gradient, on embankments, viaducts and girder bridges, in cuttings and tunnels. The painted town street grid is gone; the ground in town is yards, cobbles and gardens. The rebuild of a year's scenery is now about 0.6 to 0.8 s in a debug build on this machine, which is a visible hitch.

**Towns** (`scatter/towns.rs`): laid out along the roads through them, with high streets, back lanes and cross streets that fill in with density, houses on plots fronting the streets with windows and doors, a market square with a hall or cross, a church facing it and a station road. After docs/ART.md (a planning session's notes on visible change), a plot's place along its street is fixed, back streets go up a row at a time in rows fixed per street, a row's style is settled by the year it was first built (terraces from 1815, separate houses before), and each house steps up a storey at a density of its own. The sim itself churns town ground: about a third of urban cells switch in or out of urban use each decade in seed 7, so whole districts come and go. That wants a kept, decaying urban layer shared by the shader and the slots.

## 2026-11-09 (later): a playable first game

The viewer now plays a live game: `cargo run --release -p isostate-viewer` with no arguments makes island 7 with six families, the player's first, and opens over the player's home town with a card saying who you are and how to begin. Naming a `run.bin` still plays a replay.

How it fits together. The core gained families (branch `families`, merged): ownership of works and land, family cash in the money ledger, dividends and rents to owners, members who manage industry groups for a small efficiency bonus, AI families that invest every year, and a live API (`live_static` once, `live_year` after each simulated year). The viewer runs the simulation in its own thread and writes each finished year into the same arrays a replay reads, so the map, panels and timeline did not have to change. The thread only works when asked: the clock asks for the next year when it reaches the last one there is, and the player's orders go in between, so an order given while looking at 1786 lands before 1787 is worked out. With `n_families = 0` the core is still byte-for-byte the port of the Python.

The interface, all in the paper-and-ink style: three floating cards along the top (the family with its worth, cash, works and land; the date with Pause, 1x to 8x and Next year; tabs with little drawn icons), a scrollable side panel (Family, Holdings, Markets, News), a timeline with the major news ticked along it, a help card on H, and an inspector that splits a title from its notes. Click a square in the present and the inspector says who holds it, offers to buy it at 25 years' rent, and lists what could be built there with its cost, its expected return at today's prices, and how deep the market is (the share of the town's purchases it would make, and how far above its usual price the good sells, tagged "thin market" when either is high). The family panel has the family's worth over the years, last year's accounts, the members (click one to put them in charge of a trade) and the great families' table; holdings lists the family's works and flies the camera to them. The end of 1900 brings a card with the final standings.

On the map: family works fly a pennant in the family colour and family land is fenced in it; an ordered works gets surveyors' pegs until it goes up; the square under the pointer is outlined faintly and the clicked one boldly. Unrelated to the game but part of the same push on the look: houses now line the streets in rows before the blocks behind fill in, works stand in scaffolding the year they open or grow a size, and harbour towns have small sailing boats tacking offshore.

Is there a game? A helper wrote scripted players (`examples/bots.rs`) and ran them over four seeds. Passive play comes last every time. Greedy play (always build the best promised return) does badly: the promised returns are snapshots that ignore what the new works and its imitators do to the price, and in thin markets they are wildly high (rail forges promise 1,500% and every one dies). A prudent player who only builds where it would make at most a quarter of the town's purchases and the price is at most three times usual, and puts spare cash in land, finished first in three seeds out of four, but only climbs in the last fifty years. Works die young for everyone (median life 11 years for works founded after 1750, even with no families), because small works are dear per unit and thin markets collapse. Changes made: site options report market depth, AI families keep out of thin markets, and rivals' news is cut to their first works of each kind.

Open design points from that pass:
- Merchants pay the gate price for all output whether or not anyone buys it (from the Python), so cheap works in thin markets can be farmed for a few years. Capping what merchants pay beyond the town's demand would close it.
- `buy_land` takes any family's land at 25 years' purchase without consent. Probably wants a premium or a refusal.
- Land is priced on last year's rent, so buying ahead of a railway is cheap. That may be the intended reward for foresight.
- Family spending is fixed (60% of income plus 2% of cash a month) and is the biggest drag on the player; it could become a lever.
- Pace: the player sits low for a long time. Starting the player with a small estate rather than cash would open the game mid-table.

The ground is now painted per pixel by a shader (helper, branch `terrain-shader`, merged). It repeats the CPU's patchwork rule exactly (the hash runs in wrapping u32 maths, and "lower class if `sub_uniform*255 < t`" turns out to be "`sub_raw <= quant[t]`", one table lookup), so trees and houses still stand on matching ground: a check that casts rays against the mesh and compares classes under every pixel agrees on 99.99% of land pixels, the rest on class edges. Fields have their own crop calendars and rows, hedges are filtered to the pixel with a painted shadow, heath gets heather and gorse, the seasons run from young crops to stubble to frost, and GPU memory for the ground fell from about 31 MB to 50 KB. Known limits: class edges between neighbouring warped cells are hard steps smoothed only by SMAA, and the painted hedge shadows assume the sun stays in the south-west. Carts on the main roads went in after the docs above were written.

## 2026-11-09: the family game, first pass

Stella's design (agreed in chat): the player is a family running a conglomerate. Rival families do the same. The government is always run by one family, so losing power does not end the game, and holding power drains the company (family members serving in government are not running it), so a good player can stay in office but most will cycle in and out. Companies mostly run themselves; the player chooses industries, places, tricks and policies, maybe shady ones with risk. Historical politics (limited franchise, rotten boroughs, reform acts, Corn Laws). The player may meddle directly (public works).

Decisions for this pass:
- Build the company half first, no politics yet. Test whether steering a self-running company is fun before adding parliament.
- The family is named people with ages and skills, because the cost of office will be people.
- The design principle that gives the company half its teeth: the sim's firms are short-sighted (they build where today's prices pay), and the player is the one agent who thinks ahead (early entry down learning curves, vertical integration, land along future lines).

In flight:
- Core (helper agent, branch `families`): families with members, firm and land ownership, actions (site options, found works, buy land, assign members), AI families, reports, and a live API (`live.rs`) so the viewer can run the sim in a thread.
- Viewer, terrain shader (helper agent, branch `terrain-shader`): move the patchwork from the CPU-painted texture into a material so fields are crisp at any zoom, with seasons.
- Viewer, interface (me, branch `rust-3d`): a proper game interface in the same paper-and-ink style, then the live game loop with the player's actions on the map and family banners on buildings.
