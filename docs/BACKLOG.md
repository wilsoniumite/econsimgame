## How we work, since 19 November

- **One builder a piece, one gate.** Each piece is built by one builder on a branch of its own (`night/g-...`). It reaches `main` only through one automatic gate, the landing tool, which merges the branch in a scratch checkout, runs the core's tests with both golden tests, a sweep of eight islands and the pictures, and only then moves `main` and pushes it. Nothing reaches `main` another way. The pictures are taken and looked at; they are no longer compared pixel by pixel.
- **Chains and batches.** A line of stages that each build on the one before (the run to 2050's, the sandbox's) is built as a chain: each stage's builder starts from the branch of the stage before, and the chain reports when its last stage is done. Pieces that are ready together are merged onto one branch as a batch, where they are mended where they meet (the switch lists, the Rules tab's walk, the game's golden hashes), and the batch goes through the gate once. Two batches landed on 19 November.
- **The faults' round.** What a builder sees and leaves goes, a line a fault, to [backlog/for-the-bugfix-round.md](backlog/for-the-bugfix-round.md), and a round of mending takes them up together when the chains in flight have landed (the first step back, below, says what its first batch holds).
- **What is mended at once:** anything that breaks the build or the tests on `main`, unbalances the books, makes a run fail, or stops the game being played. Every other fault is written down in one line and waits for a later round of mending. The landing tool files each piece's faults in [backlog/for-the-bugfix-round.md](backlog/for-the-bugfix-round.md) as it lands.
- **The big features first.** The features that may be decisive come first, the run to 2050 above all (The order of work, below). Whether a piece works is judged by measuring the island, not by reading its code, and each builder reports the numbers that show it.
- **A step back, every now and then:** a review of the design, the architecture, speed, the goals and how we work, between pieces. The first was taken on 19 November (below).
- **The managing session** holds the plan and the decisions and stays out of the details. The earlier pages call it the director.

## How it is kept

- **A note goes in here first**, dated and word for word, in "The notes, as written" at the foot. It is not started because it was said.
- **Notes on one thing are gathered** and taken up together, so that a second thought can change the first before any work is done.
- **The backlog is gone through by the managing session** after landings (whenever a piece is merged into `main`) and when a round of notes arrives; the faults a landing brings are already filed by the landing tool (above). Notes the landed work has answered are closed, with where. Notes it has made moot are pruned, with a line saying why. The rest are ranked again.
- **A note is taken up when its time has come:** nothing in flight is rewriting the same code or the same part of the picture; what it depends on has landed; and it ranks above whatever else is ready. Two exceptions: what is mended at once (How we work, above), and a fix of a line or two in code nobody is working in.
- **The ranking**, highest first: what breaks play or makes the picture say something untrue; what many other things wait on; what she has raised more than once; what makes the island's economics truer; polish.
- **What helpers and reviewers find** along the way is kept on a page of its own, [backlog/found-along-the-way.md](backlog/found-along-the-way.md), ranked in the same list as her notes, and never dressed as her note. Below, a number alone is one of her notes and a number after F is one of the pieces found.

## The first step back, 19 November

Four readers looked at the island, the code, speed and how the work is done, against `main` after the second batch. What they found, and what was decided:

- **The island to 1900 holds; after 1950 it grows poorer.** All twelve standing facts hold at every stage so far. But the wage halves by 2050, hands drift back to the land (from 0.29 to 0.47 of all work), and there are far more people than the design expects (2.8 million where about 1.2 were looked for). Later stages assume growth, so their results would mislead. The land's machines are built next and judged by whether wages grow. If they do not, the stage that gives people ages and causes of death comes before anything else.
- **Nothing automatic watched 1900 to 2050.** The fixed tests and the gate stopped at 1900, and one missed target rode through three stages unreported. A guard now does, in the sweep and in the game's golden test (In flight).
- **A builder now says of each promised result whether it was met, missed or not measured,** and a chain of stages reports that, so that a miss can stop a line.
- **Each rule will be declared once,** so that the sandbox cannot throw a switch nobody has tried (today a new rule meets the sandbox's hand-kept lists only when a batch is put together). It is built on the sandbox's line after the Works tool.
- **Charts for the rules of the run to 2050 are being built,** so that they can be seen at work: work's share, owners and machines, the true wage.
- **Computing and AI are brought forward** in the order of stages once wages grow.
- **A first round of mending follows:** the long frame when a year arrives (Ready next 8), the month's long function split into phases, and the first batch of small faults from [backlog/for-the-bugfix-round.md](backlog/for-the-bugfix-round.md). ### 3. The look of 1900 to 2050 (her note of 19 November, on the look of 2050)

She had been told that the island of 2050 looks like the island of 1900 with more houses, and answered that medium-density housing, highways and skyscrapers will have to be in for 2050 to look at all right, and probably more (the note is at the foot). On 19 November three designers each took one direction and drew it with the game's own viewer, in its lab, with new generators built from the houses' kit. Every picture shows a stand-in town on flat ground, laid out from a made-up record; none is the real island. Their board and its nine best pictures are kept with the night's reports on her machine, out of the repository. The three directions:

- **An English industrial town grown on.** The town never tears itself down: the market place and church of 1780 stay in the middle, with railway terraces, a few slabs and point blocks of the 1960s, rings of semis, council pairs and builders' estates, offices and two towers inside a ring road, a motorway at the edge, and the mills by the river turned into flats. Its new houses come nearest of the three to the houses' detail; but its town is a square grid, and 2000 and 2050 look almost the same, because only the edge grows.
- **Dense and continental.** A town that cannot spread past its reach thickens instead: courtyard blocks of four to seven storeys with shops under the flats, out to a ring road sunk in a cutting, fields right outside it, the old core kept and a handful of towers on the station square. It is the warmest of the three close to and light to draw; from the game's distance it reads as a neat stamp of identical squares, more a planned capital than a grown English town, with no highways to speak of.
- **Tall and car-borne.** From across the island the town is one spike of towers set in a low carpet of detached houses, each square of suburb laid out in the street pattern of the year it joined, with a ring road, raised radials, cloverleaves, malls and sheds. It answers "skyscrapers" and "highways" most directly; but its towers are plain boxes well below the houses' detail, and the old market and church disappear under them.

Four things hold whichever is chosen. **She chooses by looking:** the board asks her eye three questions (whether towers at true height read as a town or as toys, whether the old heart is kept or let go, and whether the courtyard blocks feel cosy or regimented), and nothing that only one direction needs is built before she has answered. **The houses as drawn are never changed;** a new era's buildings stand beside them, made from the same kit at the same fineness. **Every form follows from what the simulation knows of a square** (a cell): a block, a tower or a road is drawn where the simulation's figures for that square call for it, and not where it would merely look well.

**What all three need, whichever is chosen.** These can be built now, before she chooses. The new forms go into the generators' registry with colours of their own, rather than colours mixed from the ones that exist. They are laid along the streets and closes the town's layout already makes, so that towns stop being square grids. A square is built again in the scenery only where the form of one of its lots changes. And all three need flats or blocks of flats, office towers, sheds, roads drawn in profile with cars and lorries on them, and a rule for when a lot is rebuilt.

**What else 2050 needs, beyond housing, highways and towers.** Power stations, pylons and substations; schools and hospitals; parks, squares, playing fields and street trees; cars and lorries, parked and moving, and petrol stations; sheds, shopping malls and data halls at the edge of town; lit windows and street lamps at dusk; old mills standing empty and then turned into flats; wind turbines and fields of solar panels; reservoirs and water towers; a changed countryside of fewer, larger fields, with the old spoil heaps going green; the railway electrified, some of its lines closed, a bigger station and a tram depot; and allotments, cemeteries, a football ground and a high street given over to people on foot. None of the three showed the real island with its coast and hills, the countryside of 2050, the game's far view, a town at night, or any sign of the age of computing beyond a mention of data halls, so each of those is still to be drawn for the first time.

**What the simulation must come to know first.** For each square and each year, a figure for how densely it is built and a rank for its rent; the year it was built on, running on past 1900, where the record's byte for the year a square joined its town stops today; and a count of its people that does not stop at 2,040, where the record's byte for its residents stops today. It needs the traffic each way carries, which means adding up the carting flows along each route; the record carries no traffic today. Later it needs the number of cars a household keeps. And if the stone heart of a town is to be kept, it needs some notion of a listed building. Most of this belongs with what the record carries for the viewer (Ready next 6), and the power stations with the run to 2050's stage 6, electricity.
